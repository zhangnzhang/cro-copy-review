# Scoring anchors for the 10 dimensions

Each dimension has anchors at 10 / 8 / 6 / 4 / 2 / 0 (scaled for the 15- and 5-point dimensions). Score to the nearest point; when torn between two scores, pick the anchor whose description fits better.

When a diagnosis needs data, take it from `evidence.md`: headlines (Ogilvy, Copyblogger, NN/g, CXL, Moz/Conductor) for dimensions 1 and 6, CTA tests (VWO/Veeam, Unbounce, KlientBoost) for dimension 8, specificity cases for dimensions 2 and 4, and review research (PowerReviews, Spiegel, BrightLocal, Baymard) for dimension 7.

---

## 1. Hook & Headline (10)

**What it tests:** whether the headline + subheadline answer the visitor's first question — "is this for me?" — within about 3 seconds.

**Basis:**
- The headline's job is to earn attention and set up the rest of the page. If it fails, nothing below it gets read.
- Number-led headlines ("7 ways to…") set a concrete expectation and tend to pull more clicks.
- A brand name posed as a question ("What is Acme Select?") answers nothing for someone who has never heard of the brand.

**Anchors:**
- **10** — In 3 seconds it's clear who it's for and what they get; the headline is short (roughly 6–12 words); the subheadline adds new information instead of restating it.
- **8** — The core message is there but the hook is a little soft, or the subheadline runs long.
- **6** — Understandable but forgettable; no concrete hook.
- **4** — Too abstract ("Redefining clean", "Innovative design") or too long.
- **2** — Doesn't say what's being sold, or the subheadline just repeats the headline.
- **0** — No headline hierarchy at all.

**Common deductions:** brand-name headlines; subheadline restating the headline; SHOUTING IN TITLE CASE OR CAPS (see dimension 6).

## 2. Benefit > Feature (15 — highest weight)

**What it tests:** whether the copy talks about what the reader **gets** (outcomes, feelings, change) or what the product **has** (specs, parts, settings). This is the most common and most costly copy mistake, hence 15 points.

**Basis:**
- The classic example: "This vacuum has a 2000W motor" (feature) vs "Spend less time cleaning and more time with your family" (benefit).
- Readers don't buy features; they buy the problem those features solve.
- Vitamin vs painkiller framing: vitamins sell the slow, compounding good; painkillers sell the pain going away now.

**Anchors:**
- **15** — Every feature is translated into an outcome; written as "you'll…" or as a scene ("your Saturdays back").
- **12** — Mostly benefits; 1–2 bare specs left untranslated.
- **9** — Half features, half benefits; features still lead.
- **6** — Mostly features with an occasional vague benefit.
- **3** — Pure spec list ("2000W, HEPA 13, cordless, 5 speeds").
- **0** — Specs plus empty claims ("premium", "professional").

**Common deductions:** untranslated materials ("304 stainless steel" → "won't rust in ten years"); empty benefits ("high quality", "innovative design"); model-number names instead of what changed ("X1 Pro 2.0" → "the quieter one").

| Feature (don't) | Benefit (do) |
|---|---|
| 2000W motor | The whole house in 12 minutes |
| HEPA 13 filter | Keeps allergens out of your air |
| 304 stainless steel | Still rust-free in ten years |
| AI object detection | It learns the room; you don't have to |

*(Numbers in example rewrites are illustrative. In real copy, use only figures you can back up.)*

## 3. Emotional Resonance (10)

**What it tests:** whether the copy triggers an emotion, uses emotional rather than rational words, and — on mobile — commits to **one** emotion instead of trying to hit them all.

**Basis:**
- Swap rational words for emotional ones, and match words to how the product feels: a lemon soda is "sparkling, bright"; a mahogany desk is "solid, weighty, permanent".
- Tone isn't what you say but how you say it.
- Short mobile copy can't carry several emotional appeals at once; pick one.

**Anchors:**
- **10** — One clear emotion; emotional vocabulary that matches the product.
- **8** — The emotion lands, but some word choices are flat or rational.
- **6** — Some feeling, nothing stands out; neutral vocabulary.
- **4** — Purely rational: specs and functions.
- **2** — Mobile copy chasing 3+ emotions, none of them landing.
- **0** — No emotional touchpoint; reads like a spec sheet.

**Six emotions to choose from (pick one for mobile):**
- **Caring** — family, health, pets, baby
- **Novelty** — trends, tech, limited editions
- **Exclusivity** — premium, membership, collectibles
- **Reassurance** — insurance, health, education, high-trust categories
- **Pain** — problem-solving (painkiller) products
- **Urgency** — seasonal, time-limited, genuinely scarce

## 4. Specificity (10)

**What it tests:** concrete numbers, $ and % versus empty adjectives ("fast", "great", "life-changing").

**Basis:**
- Specific claims are more believable and more memorable than general ones: "set up in under 10 minutes" beats "quick setup".
- Round big numbers so a brain can hold them: $2,632,024 → $2.6 million (one of Roy Peter Clark's tips for complex information in *Writing Tools*).
- Put the number next to the claim it supports, not in a footnote.

**Anchors:**
- **10** — Every core claim has a specific number, $ or %; big numbers are rounded; numbers sit next to their claims.
- **8** — Specific, with 1–2 lingering "fast / great / premium".
- **6** — The odd number; mostly qualitative.
- **4** — Wall-to-wall adjectives ("professional, leading, efficient").
- **2** — Unrounded big numbers nobody can parse ("used 1,247,389 times").
- **0** — No numbers at all.

**Common fixes:** "Fast shipping" → "Order by 3pm, ships same day" · "Durable" → "2-year warranty, free replacement" · "Loved by customers" → "4.8/5 from [real review count] reviews".

## 5. Voice of Customer (10)

**What it tests:** whether the copy uses **customers' words** or **internal jargon** — whether it came out of the buyer's head or the marketing team's.

**Basis:**
- Conversion copywriters (Joanna Wiebe of Copyhackers is the best-known advocate) argue the most persuasive copy is lifted from how customers describe their own problem — not written from scratch.
- Readers have seen industry jargon a thousand times and tune it out.
- Review mining: read real reviews of your product and competitors', collect phrases people repeat, and use them verbatim.

**Anchors:**
- **10** — Lines map to things real customers have said; second person ("you"); zero jargon.
- **8** — Customer voice with 1–2 brand-speak slips.
- **6** — Neutral; half customer, half brand.
- **4** — Reads like the product manual ("equipped with… featuring… utilizing…").
- **2** — Jargon throughout ("empower", "leverage", "best-in-class", "solutions").
- **0** — Internal strategy-deck language.

**If this scores low, recommend review mining:**
1. Read reviews on marketplaces, review sites, Reddit and forums for your category.
2. Mark 3–5 situations or pain phrases people repeat.
3. Put those exact phrases into the copy. Don't polish them away.

## 6. Rhythm & Readability (10)

**What it tests:** varied sentence rhythm, skimmability, mobile fit, sentence case, active voice.

**Basis:**
- Use strong verbs. Parenthetical asides, fragments and the occasional one-word sentence add rhythm.
- Sentence case reads friendlier than Title Case ([Making a case for letter case](https://medium.com/@jsaito/making-a-case-for-letter-case)).
- Active voice over passive.
- Short bullets let the copy breathe; on mobile, use short lines, "read more" and tabs; tight spacing causes cognitive strain.

**Anchors:**
- **10** — Short/medium/long sentences alternate; bullets where useful; sentence case; active voice; comfortable on a phone.
- **8** — Good rhythm with one Title Case or passive slip.
- **6** — Every sentence the same length; bullets that read like paragraphs.
- **4** — Long unbroken paragraphs; tiring on mobile.
- **2** — Long compound sentences + Title Case + passive voice.
- **0** — Unreadable on a phone.

**Case check:** ✅ "Get my free trial" · ❌ "Get My Free Trial" · ❌ "GET MY FREE TRIAL"
**Voice check:** ✅ "We reply within 24 hours" · ❌ "Your inquiry will be responded to within 24 hours"

## 7. Trust & Social Proof (10)

**What it tests:** at least one of the three types of social proof (statistics, examples, quotes); risk reversal (guarantee, refund, trial); real scarcity stated, fake scarcity absent.

**Basis:**
- Three types of social proof: **statistics** ("used by 40,000 teams"), **examples** ("used by [well-known customer]"), **quotes** (a named customer's words).
- Endorsements work because they aren't persuasion — they show other people already chose this.
- If quantities really are limited, say so. Never invent scarcity.

**Anchors:**
- **10** — 2+ types of social proof, risk reversal, and scarcity (if any) is real.
- **8** — One type + risk reversal.
- **6** — One type, no risk reversal.
- **4** — "Loved by thousands" with no evidence.
- **2** — Fake scarcity (fake countdown, fake "only 2 left").
- **0** — No trust elements and fake scarcity.

**Risk reversal examples:** 60-day money-back guarantee · free returns · try before you buy · "If you don't love it, we'll refund you — no questions asked."

**Never fabricate proof.** In rewrites, use placeholders like `[real customer count]` or `[verified customer quote]` and ask the owner to fill them in.

## 8. CTA Clarity (10)

**What it tests:** verb-led CTAs that say **what happens after the click** and what the reader gets.

**Basis:**
- **Documented test:** Veeam changed a product-page link from "Request a quote" to "Request pricing". Click-through to the sales inquiry page rose from 0.54% to 1.40% (+161.66%) — [VWO case study](https://static.wingify.com/vwo/uploads/2019/06/veeam-software-increased-conversions_2019-06-29-19-30-54.pdf). Readers knew exactly what they'd get (a price) instead of facing a vague process (a quote).
- "Sign me up" feels more personal than a curt "Sign up".

**Anchors:**
- **10** — Verb first + clear benefit + first-person feel ("Get my free guide").
- **8** — Verb + clear benefit, slightly stiff ("Get free guide").
- **6** — Verb-led, benefit vague ("Sign up now").
- **4** — Bare CTA ("Submit", "Click here", "Learn more").
- **2** — Fear framing ("Don't miss out").
- **0** — Doesn't say what it does ("OK").

| Weak | Stronger |
|---|---|
| Submit | Get my free quote |
| Click here | Start my 30-day trial |
| Learn more | Show me how it works |
| Sign up | Sign me up — $5 off my first order |
| Buy now | Add to cart — ships today |
| Request a quote | Request pricing |
| Subscribe | Subscribe — one email a week, no spam |

## 9. Framework Application (10)

**What it tests:** a recognizable structure (PAS, AIDA, a catchy-intro formula, or the standard product-page flow) and whether each information sentence survives "So what?".

**Basis:**
- **PAS:** Problem → Agitation → Solution.
- **AIDA:** Attention → Interest → Desire → Action.
- **Catchy intro** (popularized by Derek Halpern of Social Triggers): benefit-led title → a question that builds interest → a promise of what the reader will get.
- **So what? test:** ask "so what?" of each sentence; if the honest answer is "nothing", cut it.

**Anchors:**
- **10** — The structure is obvious; every information sentence passes "So what?".
- **8** — Structure present but understated; a few sentences fail "So what?".
- **6** — Has a beginning, middle and end, loosely.
- **4** — A pile of features.
- **2** — No logical chain.
- **0** — Contradicts itself (e.g. "luxury" and "cheapest" in the same breath).

**Product page flow:** Hero (headline + subheadline) → top 3 benefits → social proof → FAQ → risk reversal → CTA.

## 10. Comparison & Metaphor (5)

**What it tests:** whether an "It's like X, but for Y" line or a sharp analogy makes an unfamiliar idea instantly clear.

**Basis:**
- "It's like X, but for Y" anchors something new to something known ("Uber for X").
- Metaphors and similes turn abstract qualities into felt ones: not "stable structure" but "steady as bedrock".

**Anchors:**
- **5** — A clear X-but-for-Y or a vivid analogy that makes the idea click.
- **4** — An analogy that's fine but not sharp.
- **3** — An attempt with a vague reference point.
- **2** — No comparison or analogy (the default for everyday products — not a serious flaw).
- **1** — A misleading analogy that confuses more than it explains.

This dimension matters most for **new categories** and **abstract services**. For everyday products, 2–3 is normal.

---

## Overall score

- **90–100** — Textbook; barely needs changes.
- **75–89** — Strong; small edits.
- **60–74** — Passable; 1–2 clear problems.
- **45–59** — Several problems; rewrite recommended.
- **30–44** — Actively hurting conversion; must change.
- **0–29** — Working against itself.

In the one-line verdict, name **the dimension that is the biggest bottleneck**. That guides the fix better than the total does.
